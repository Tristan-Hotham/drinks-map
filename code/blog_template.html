Champagne, Cognac, Rioja, Tequila: drinks are named after places more than almost anything else we buy. I tried to put every drink back where it came from, on one map, and found that the map says as much about who keeps the records as about who drinks.

After mapping what the world eats, the obvious next question was what it drinks. Wine and its appellations, beer and its styles, cider and perry, spirits of every kind, sake and the other rice drinks, and the old traditional drinks such as kvass, pulque, chicha and palm wine. Plus the brands, the people who make them, and the drink each country holds up as its own.

No single dataset covers all of that, so the map combines four open sources. Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, gives {{drinks.n|int}} named drinks with a country. The EU's register of protected names gives {{gi.n|int}} wines, spirits, beers and ciders whose names are protected in law. OpenStreetMap's volunteer mappers have tagged {{producers.osm_n|int}} wineries, breweries and distilleries. And two Wikipedia lists name the national drink of {{national.n_drink|int}} countries and the national liquor of {{national.n_liquor|int}}.

The national drink is often tea

Start with the pub question: what is your country's national drink? Wikipedia's list is a crowd's answer, and it is more sober than you might expect. Of the {{national.n_drink|int}} countries in it, {{national.first_nonalc|int}} ({{national.first_nonalc_pct|pc}}) list a drink without alcohol first: tea in {{national.first_tea|int}} countries, coffee in {{national.first_coffee|int}}, and everything else from mate in Argentina and lassi in India to kava across the Pacific.

Among countries that name an alcoholic drink first, spirits of every kind lead ({{national.first_spirits|int}} countries), brandies and fruit spirits most of all, then beer ({{national.first_kind.beer|int}}) and cocktails such as Brazil's caipirinha ({{national.first_kind.cocktail|int}}). The separate list of national liquors reads like a map of distilling traditions: rakija across the Balkans, arak around the eastern Mediterranean, rum in the Caribbean, baijiu in China, soju in Korea, shochu in Japan, tequila and mezcal in Mexico, and pisco claimed by both Peru and Chile. Across both lists, the most named drink is rum ({{national.most_named.0.count|int}} countries).

Wine: a continent of protected names

Wine is where drink and place are welded together by law. The EU register holds {{gi.by_cat.wine|int}} protected wine names: Italy has {{gi.top_wine.0.n|int}}, France {{gi.top_wine.1.n|int}}, Spain {{gi.top_wine.2.n|int}} and Greece {{gi.top_wine.3.n|int}}. I could put {{gi.placed|int}} of the {{gi.n|int}} protected drink names on the map, through the place Wikidata links to each one or a place of the same name in the same country. The region with the most is {{gi.wine_top_regions.0.name|raw}} ({{gi.wine_top_regions.0.n|int}}), home of Burgundy's village appellations.

On the ground, OpenStreetMap has {{producers.osm_by_group.wine|int}} wineries. France ({{producers.osm_top_wine.0.n|int}}) and Germany ({{producers.osm_top_wine.1.n|int}}) lead, and the busiest single region is {{producers.osm_topreg_wine.0.name|raw}} ({{producers.osm_topreg_wine.0.n|int}}). That says something about small family estates along German rivers, and something about how keen German mappers are.

Two maps of beer

Beer is where the sources part company. Wikidata knows {{drinks.by_group.beer|int}} named beers, almost all individual products, and {{drinks.beer_top3_pct|pc}} of them come from three countries: the United States, the Netherlands and Belgium. That is not a ranking of beer cultures. It is a ranking of who has typed beers into Wikidata: the makers with the most entries are small brewers such as {{drinks.beer_top_makers.0.name|raw}}, with {{drinks.beer_top_makers.0.n|int}} beers each listed one by one.

The breweries tell a broader story: {{producers.osm_by_group.beer|int}} of them in OpenStreetMap, led by the United States, France, the United Kingdom and Germany, with {{producers.osm_topreg_beer.0.name|raw}} the busiest region ({{producers.osm_topreg_beer.0.n|int}}). Protected beer names are rare, just {{gi.by_cat.beer|int}}, {{cards.beer_cze_deu|int}} of them Czech or German.

Spirits, sake and the old drinks

Spirits are the most varied family and the most thinly recorded. The register protects {{gi.by_cat.spirits|int}} spirit names, {{gi.spirit_families.brandy|int}} of them brandies and fruit spirits: Bulgarian grozdova rakya, Hungarian pálinka, Italian grappa. OpenStreetMap has {{producers.osm_by_group.spirits|int}} distilleries, and the region with the most is {{producers.osm_topreg_spirits.0.name|raw}} ({{producers.osm_topreg_spirits.0.n|int}}).

Some of the oldest drinks leave the faintest trace. Wikidata lists {{producers.sake_wikidata|int}} sake breweries, {{producers.sake_top.0.n|int}} of them in Japan. Cider and perry have {{gi.cider_n|int}} protected names, from Normandy and Asturias to Herefordshire. Kvass, pulque, chicha, tej, boza and palm wine appear in Wikidata as kinds of drink with a country, and almost never as anything more: much of what they are is made at home or sold in markets, not catalogued.

A map of who keeps the records

Put every source together and Europe holds {{records.europe_pct|pc}} of the {{records.n|int}} records, North America {{records.by_continent.1.pct|pc}}, and Africa {{records.africa_pct|f1}}%. Read naively, the map would say the Netherlands is a greater beer nation than Germany and that Africa barely drinks. Read honestly, it shows where open data about drinks is rich and where it is silent.

Wikidata knows {{drinks.top_beer.0.n|int}} American beers by name, and Ethiopian tej only as a word.

How I did this

I fetched everything in a browser on {{meta.retrieved|raw}}. From Wikidata I took every type of alcoholic drink and every named drink below it, plus alcohol brands and a few drinks Wikidata does not connect to the rest (rum, kvass, kumis and tuba), and gave each a family from Wikidata's own class tree. Each drink is placed in its country of origin, and on the map at its own coordinates, its location's or its maker's. Cocktails are left out. Protected names come from the EU's eAmbrosia register and are placed through Wikidata, partly by matching names, so a few dots will sit on a namesake village. Producers come from Wikidata and OpenStreetMap, and national drinks from Wikipedia's two lists. The paper sets out every step and caveat.

This is a map of where drinks come from and what the open record says about them. It counts records, not litres, it does not rank anything, and nothing in it is a recommendation to drink. No logos are used.
